# JACK — ingestion, monitoring, data quality

*You own: `src/capture.py`, `src/load_raw.py`, `src/check_quality.py`, `helpers/dq.py`,
the GitHub Actions workflow, and the 08-05 recovery.*

### Q: Walk me through the 2026-08-05 incident.
The source sent prices as text — `$157.12` instead of `157.12` — for all 155 rows.
It passed three separate checks:
1. **Missing-value check:** a `$` isn't a null, so it counted zero missing.
2. **The parser:** `pd.to_numeric(errors="coerce")` turned every price into NaN silently.
3. **Reconciliation:** the totals matched, because the same nulls were on both sides.

Result: $56,970.09 of revenue read as $0.00 while units sold stayed positive. **No check
fired.** We found it by grepping raw files during a pre-submission audit.

We recovered every cent from the preserved raw file — computed by hand first, then the
fixed pipeline produced the identical figure independently. Then we fixed both halves:
`_clean_numeric_str()` strips `$` and `,` at the single point every numeric column flows
through, and `_nonnumeric()` counts values that are present but unparseable.

### Q: How do you know the fix actually works?
We attacked it. `deck/poison_fixture.py` pushes a synthetic file with five planted `$`
prices through the real gate and the real parser. Executed 2026-08-08 at commit
`1c4884f`; the gate flagged `nonnumeric(unit_price=5)` and the parser recovered all five
to the cent. Log and metadata are committed in `deck/out/fixture/`. *We test the fix
rather than trust it.*

### Q: Why didn't you retry the 404 on 08-06?
A 404 means the file isn't there. Retrying produces three identical failures and a
longer log. Timeouts and connection resets look different in the response and would
deserve their own handling — we've never seen one. The run is logged `status=failed`
with the captured error string, and the next day succeeded with 125 rows.

### Q: Why not backfill the missing day from 08-05?
Because it would work. Every downstream number would still reconcile, the dashboard
would look healthy, and nobody would know 08-06 never arrived. **A gap in the data is a
fact, and the analysis should see it.** `daily_sales` has no 08-06 row on purpose.

### Q: Why is your ingestion log a CSV and not a database table?
It's append-only, it survives a full database rebuild, and anyone can open it in Excel
and compare today to yesterday. It's also mirrored into the `ingestion_log` SQL table on
every load, so it's queryable too — the CSV stays the source of truth.

### Q (uncomfortable): 28 of your 34 log rows share one timestamp. Was this really running daily?
Yes, and we documented this rather than let someone discover it. Those 28 rows are a
single backfill executed when we consolidated into this repository on 08-03. Scheduled
per-day capture *in this repo* starts 08-04, and each of those rows is corroborated by a
`github-actions[bot]` commit at the same timestamp to the second. The 28 backfilled
files came from three separate collectors — the predecessor repo plus two private backup
repos we ran as insurance, which is how we recovered 07-21 and 07-22 when the main cron
missed them. **Hedge honestly:** we can't verify the predecessor's run history from
inside this repo, and we say so in `DECISIONS.md`.

### Q (uncomfortable): The 08-05 quality log row still shows a clean file. Why not regenerate it?
Deliberate. That row — `na_unit_price=0`, no flag — *is* the evidence that no check
fired at the time. Regenerating the log would replace the record of the miss with a
clean row and erase the incident. Instead `src/verify_dq_history.py` re-runs the current
gate over all history in memory and diffs it against the log, writing nothing. It
reports 08-05 as the one known difference and finds no undocumented ones.

### Q: What breaks first if this scales?
Two things, and we know both:
1. **Idempotency is keyed on the date**, so the design assumes one file per day. An
   intraday or replayed feed breaks that model.
2. **Reorder-proof is not rename-proof.** We read columns by name, so the 08-07 column
   shuffle was a non-event. But if a column were *renamed*, `load_raw.py` backfills the
   missing expected column with NULLs instead of raising — so a renamed price column
   would load a silent NULL-revenue day and only surface downstream.

### Q: Why keep the raw files at all — isn't the database enough?
The 08-05 recovery is the entire answer. The database had already lost that revenue.
The original bytes were the only place it still existed, and it took about fifteen
minutes to get back. Raw-first is cheap insurance that paid out once in 32 days.

# BURHAN — SQL modeling, entity resolution, the repo

*You own: `sql/01_schema.sql`, `src/crosswalk.py`, `src/transform.py`, the dashboard,
and the limitations slide.*

### Q: P1076 matched at 0.50 name similarity against a 0.60 floor. Explain.
**The floor applies to the composite, not the name.** P1076's name scored 0.50, but
subclass matched exactly (1.00) and price closeness was 0.841:

`0.6 × 0.50 + 0.2 × 1.00 + 0.2 × 0.841 = 0.668`

That clears 0.60 by 0.068. Its attribute block had exactly one candidate. And because
name similarity was under the 0.85 confidence threshold, it came out **medium** and
landed in the review queue. *The system flagged its own thin accept.*

> Do **not** call P1076 "the weakest accept" — that's P1072 at 0.657, and the weakest
> accepted row overall is a possible_match at 0.642. The 0.668 and 0.068 are correct.

### Q: 72 matched but 18 need review. Which is it?
Two orthogonal axes. **Status** is the decision made; **confidence** is the strength of
the name evidence. 10 of the 72 matched rows are medium-confidence: committed
downstream, queued for audit. 76 products carry a new ID; 18 carry a doubt flag; 14 are
in both groups.

### Q: Why 0.60? That looks arbitrary.
It is a judgment call and we say so. Accepted and rejected candidate scores **overlap
between 0.642 and 0.939** — the two populations are not cleanly separated, so no
threshold splits them without trading false matches against missed ones. We set the cut
where a human reviewer could still work through what fell near it.

### Q: Why isn't brand in the matching model?
Deliberately held out so it could validate the result independently. If brand were in
the score, checking the mapping against brand would just be the model agreeing with
itself. Held out, it's a real test: three brands were relabeled in the migration, so a
literal string comparison fails on 28 of 76 pairs — but the *mapping* is clean, 8 legacy
brands to 8 new labels, one-to-one, with no product crossing brands.

### Q: You planted decoys? How did the matcher do?
The new catalog contains four literal "… Alt" twins. All four landed in
`possible_match` — P1077, P1078, P1079, P1080 — flagged because the top two candidates
scored within 0.05 of each other. That's exactly the intended behavior: high enough to
be worth a look, not high enough to accept quietly.

### Q: Your schema declares primary keys and foreign keys. Are they enforced?
**No, and this is the thing we'd fix first.** Eight of ten tables are written with
`to_sql(if_exists="replace")`, which drops the table and recreates it from the DataFrame
schema — discarding every constraint. Verified by reading `sqlite_master`. Only
`raw_orders` (appended) and `ingestion_log` (delete-then-append) keep their DDL.

Every correctness guarantee we designed into SQL is enforced in Python instead. The
pipeline produces correct results, but the database would accept an incorrect write. The
140 duplicate rows from the stale file were caught by transform logic; the declared
UNIQUE constraint would have rejected them at insert and didn't exist to do so.
**Fix:** create from DDL and append, rather than replace.

### Q: If nothing blocks on the review flag, what is it for?
It's an audit deliverable. The alternative was block-and-lose-revenue. We chose
flag-and-proceed with the cost stated: 14 of the 18 flagged rows flow through unblocked.
The twin-swap risk is bounded to the 4 `possible_match` rows; the 10 matched-medium
carry a different and smaller risk — a false positive in a single-candidate block. Not
zero, and we don't claim zero.

### Q (uncomfortable): You had a $930 gap nobody noticed. What happened?
Product `NP9999` appears in four order lines on 07-30 and exists in neither catalog, so
it has no crosswalk row. The pipeline kept the revenue and labelled the category
UNKNOWN rather than dropping it — that part worked. **The quality check also fired**:
the 07-30 log row records `orphan_product=4` with the `orphans` flag. Nobody acted on
it. We rediscovered it by tracing why the dashboard total differed from the
reconciliation figure. *The detection worked; the follow-through didn't, and that's the
part of the monitoring loop we'd fix first.*

### Q (uncomfortable): Any problems from committing the database to git?
Yes — we hit it ourselves. A binary database in git isn't mergeable. Two of us rebuilt
it while prepping this deck and git refused the merge; restore-and-rerun was the
workaround. That's our own limitation biting us in practice. We accepted it because
GitHub Actions can't reach the campus MySQL server behind the VPN, and the stated cost
is that SQLite-in-repo is not a guaranteed single source of truth.

# JAMES — weather enrichment

*You own: `src/weather.py`, the Open-Meteo integration, the cache, and `weather_daily`.*

### Q: Why Open-Meteo?
Free, no API key, and it serves the ERA5 reanalysis archive — the same
government-grade dataset used in climate research — at daily resolution for any
coordinate. It also caches cleanly, so a fresh clone reproduces the whole pipeline
offline from committed JSON.

### Q: How does weather attach to sales?
At **store-day grain**: one weather row per store per calendar date, joined on
(store, date). Each store has its own latitude and longitude from the reference table,
so a store in one city gets that city's weather, not a chain average. Timezone is set to
`America/New_York` so a "rain day" matches the selling day customers actually
experienced.

### Q: You have 1,367 sales rows. Why do you say the sample is 232?
Because weather varies by store and date — not by product category. The sales table is
finer-grained than that: every category row within a store-day inherits the same single
weather reading. So 8 stores × 29 dates = **232 independent weather observations**, and
1,367 rows is not 1,367 pieces of evidence about weather. Treating them as independent
would overstate our confidence, so we don't.

### Q: Why 29 dates and not 32?
The three missing dates are the incident days: 07-24's file was a re-send of 07-23 and
was quarantined, 08-03 arrived empty, and 08-06 returned a 404. The weather grain wears
the incident timeline.

### Q: What's the lag?
The ERA5 archive trails reality by a few days, so the newest order dates can join with
NULL weather until a later run backfills them. At lock there were zero NULL rows — the
mechanism is real, the tail happened to be empty.

### Q (uncomfortable): Did you have a bug in the cache?
Yes, and it's the most interesting thing I found. The cache key is the date range —
`S001_2026-07-07_2026-08-08.json` — and the range grows every time a new order date
arrives. The original code deleted the store's previous cache file before writing the
new one, so **every single run destroyed the previous pull.**

That matters because Open-Meteo revises its archive. Comparing the locked snapshot to
today: **five store-days have revised precipitation, and two of them crossed our rain
threshold** — 08-07 at S007 went 2.3mm → 0.9mm (rain to dry), and 08-07 at S008 went
0.4mm → 3.7mm (dry to rain). Both are the newest date at lock time, which is exactly how
a preliminary ERA5 value behaves before it settles.

We found it in audit, removed the deletion, and pulls accumulate now the way raw order
files do. **What that doesn't do is bring anything back** — the revisions above happened
while the purge was live. The deck reproduces only because it's pinned to a database
snapshot, which is luck rather than design.

### Q: So are your weather numbers wrong?
No — they're *dated*, and we can say exactly by how much. The channel finding moves from
+3.7 to +3.4 points on today's data and stays significant. The revenue finding we
already rejected stays rejected. Nothing changes direction. That's the delta card.

### Q: Why not use a weather forecast instead of history?
The question is what already happened to sales, so the archive is right for the
analysis. The *recommendation* runs on the forecast — you staff for rain before it
rains, not after — and that's a deliberate split between measuring and acting.

# CONNOR — business analysis, findings, the deck

*You own: the findings, the recommendation, the sensitivity testing, and the deck.*

### Q: Your pickup shift is p = .006. Why do you only call it "suggestive"?
Because the test assumes independent observations and ours aren't. Orders within the
same store-day share the same weather, the same staffing, the same local conditions.
That non-independence shrinks the effective sample below 3,721 and widens the true
standard error beyond what the z-test assumes. The direction and the size are stable
across every cutoff we tested; the *certainty* is capped by the design, so I won't quote
the p-value as if it were the whole story.

### Q: Why present a result that failed?
**The sensitivity test is the result.** A number that only holds at one threshold isn't
a finding, it's an artifact of where you drew the line. We had a clean-looking +13.9%
rain-day revenue lift at 1mm with 6 of 8 stores agreeing. Move the cutoff: +8.3% at 5mm
with 4 of 8, +18.9% at 10mm with 4 of 8, +9.5% at 0.4mm with 3 of 8. The estimate swings
and the cross-store agreement collapses. We killed it rather than report the threshold
that flattered it — the same rule the crosswalk enforces with its explicit floor.

### Q: Couldn't that just be store mix?
Partly, and that's the second reason we dropped it. The rainiest store, S001, has rain
on 86% of days *and* the highest median daily revenue at $6,673. The driest, S006, has
14% rain days and $4,714. Pooling across stores lets that difference masquerade as a
rain effect. Spearman across the 8 stores is 0.36 — suggestive of exactly that
confound, at p = .38 with n = 8, which is far too small to prove either way.

### Q: Why medians instead of averages?
Store-day revenue is skewed — a handful of big days would let averages manufacture a
lift that the typical day doesn't show. The live dashboard uses means, which is why it
prints slightly different numbers, and we documented that side by side rather than
quietly picking whichever looked better.

### Q: Why is rain "more than 1mm" and not "1mm or more"?
Three store-days sit at exactly 1.0mm — S004 on 07-09, S008 on 07-20, S005 on 08-05 — so
the operator alone moves which days count as rainy. We fixed one convention, disclosed
it, and checked it doesn't matter where it counts: the two pilot stores are the same
under either operator.

### Q: You ran a lot of tests. Did you go fishing?
We ran 16 — 8 stores × 2 weather variables — and reported that number on the slide.
Exactly one came back significant: S001 against temperature, rho −0.43, p = .021. **One
hit in 16 tests is what chance produces at this sample size.** Reporting it as a finding
would be the fishing.

### Q: So what should the business actually do?
Staff and pre-stage pickup capacity when rain is in the forecast. That's the one
recommendation that survives everything we killed, because it depends only on the
channel shift — the finding that held at every cutoff — and needs no claim about total
demand. Concretely: for two weeks, on forecast-rain days, move one associate to pickup
staging at the two rainiest stores, S001 and S002. Measure pickup share and pickup wait
time; scale only if share moves at least 2 points, otherwise stop. **It's a
reallocation, not a spend** — which is why it's worth doing on a suggestive finding.

### Q: Why a 2-point bar?
Pre-registered before the pilot runs, and set below the 3.7 points we observed to allow
for attenuation, but above noise. Wait time is the guardrail — a share gain that comes
with worse service isn't a win.

### Q: What would you do with more data?
232 store-days is one summer month. A full season would let us test the channel shift
*within* each store over time, which is what actually kills the store-mix confound
instead of just flagging it. That's the next step, not more slicing of this month.

### Q: The dashboard shows different numbers than your slides.
By design, and it's documented. The board reads the live table and uses `≥1mm` with
means; the slides use strict `>1mm` with medians over the frozen window. The pickup
shift holds either way — +3.7 on the slides, +3.8 on the board. The revenue lift doesn't
hold under either, which is the point of that slide. Reconciliation table is in
`DECISIONS.md` section 5.

### Q: What's the honest headline?
We built a pipeline that catches its own mistakes, and it caught six failures in 32 days
while losing zero dollars. The one weather effect that survived testing is that rain
changes *how* people buy, not *how much* — and the effect we most wanted to report is
the one we killed.
